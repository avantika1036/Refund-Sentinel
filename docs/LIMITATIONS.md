## 1. Synthetic data

The benchmark is generated by a controlled simulator.

### Why this is useful

Synthetic data gives the project:

- exact labels;
- repeatability;
- deliberately difficult legitimate controls;
- controllable abuse mechanisms;
- leakage testing;
- deterministic reproduction.

### Why this is not production evidence

Real merchants have:

- merchant-specific refund policies;
- noisy identifiers;
- product and category effects;
- shipping failures;
- customer-service intervention;
- regional differences;
- changing customer behavior;
- adaptive attackers.

Therefore:

> **The benchmark demonstrates that the system behaves as designed on a controlled dataset. It does not estimate production performance.**

---

## 2. Generalization

The benchmark includes held-out mechanisms and legitimate lookalikes, but the model has not been evaluated across a broad corpus of real merchants.

A model that performs well on a simulator can still fail when the real-world feature distribution changes.

Future validation should use:

- merchant-specific historical splits;
- time-based validation;
- cross-merchant validation;
- stress tests under changing refund policies;
- analyst-reviewed labels.

---

## 3. Model calibration

The model returns values in `[0, 1]`, but that does not establish that `0.8` means “80% probability of abuse” in the real world.

The current project does not present a formal calibration study.

The UI therefore treats ML probability as:

> **an additional analytical signal**

rather than ground truth.

Production rollout would require calibration assessment and monitoring.

---

## 4. Evasion

A coordinated actor can attempt to alter observable behavior:

- rotate devices;
- rotate addresses;
- change refund reasons;
- delay refund timing;
- vary amounts;
- reduce activity density;
- distribute behavior across more accounts.

Refund Sentinel reduces dependence on any one signal, but it is not evasion-proof.

The project deliberately avoids making that claim.

---

## 5. Graph and identifier coverage

The current structural graph materially uses supported relationships around customers, devices, addresses, payments, orders and refunds.

The graph does not provide exhaustive coverage of every identity or network signal a production risk system might have.

Consequences include:

- rings using unsupported identifiers may appear as weakly connected or disconnected;
- high-degree shared identifiers can create very large components;
- missing optional attributes reduce structural evidence.

A production graph should add explicit controls for high-degree identifiers, tenant boundaries, graph pruning and lifecycle-aware retention.

---

## 6. Cold start

New customers may have little history:

- few orders;
- few refunds;
- no meaningful historical rate;
- insufficient velocity window;
- limited relationship context.

The system can still use available lifecycle and structural signals, but confidence may be lower.

Production systems should expose confidence / evidence availability clearly and avoid turning missing history into automatic suspicion.

---

## 7. Exposure metrics

### Pending exposure

Pending exposure is potentially actionable, not guaranteed preventable. Processing can complete before an investigator responds.

### Realized suspicious amount

Already-processed refund value is retrospective context. It should not be called recoverable money without a separate recovery process.

### Current benchmark exposure capture

The persisted `abuse_exposure_capture_rate` answers how much labeled abuse refund amount belongs to positive predictions.

It does not simulate:

- analyst response latency;
- refund cancellation;
- recovery;
- chargeback outcomes;
- business interruption.

### Important wording

Use:

> “captured abuse exposure”

Do not use:

> “money prevented”

unless a separate intervention simulator actually proves it.

---

## 8. False-positive economics

The project includes configurable false-positive cost sensitivity, but the values such as ₹50, ₹200 and ₹500 are scenario assumptions.

They are not observed merchant economics.

A production deployment should estimate costs from:

- analyst time;
- customer friction;
- support contacts;
- refund delay;
- lost conversion;
- escalation costs.

---

## 9. LLM limitations

The LLM is external, probabilistic software.

Potential failures include:

- provider rate limits;
- provider model availability;
- network errors;
- timeouts;
- malformed JSON;
- provider-side behavior changes;
- transient service outages.

The current implementation mitigates these by:

- validating the response structure;
- validating entity references;
- keeping the evidence bundle authoritative;
- falling back to a deterministic narrative;
- avoiding permanent caching of failed-provider fallbacks.

### Important boundary

The LLM does not make the financial decision.

---

## 10. Prompt injection and untrusted text

Customer-provided text may contain malicious instructions such as attempts to override system behavior.

The architecture treats customer text as untrusted data and sends the LLM a structured evidence representation rather than allowing customer text to become privileged instructions.

This reduces but does not eliminate prompt-injection risk.

Production deployments should add stronger isolation and provider-specific safety controls.

---

## 11. Webhook and event-system limitations

The prototype exercises idempotency and out-of-order handling conceptually and through tests, but production systems would require stronger guarantees around:

- durable queues;
- replay handling;
- dead-letter workflows;
- event retention;
- ordering metadata;
- exactly-once business effects where required.

A webhook delivery is not itself proof that the business state has reached a final terminal state.

---

## 12. Authentication and authorization

The prototype includes API-key style development protection and webhook secret handling.

It is not a full production identity system.

Missing production-grade concerns include:

- user identity;
- role-based access control;
- merchant isolation;
- case-level authorization;
- audit policy administration;
- key rotation lifecycle.

---

## 13. Production data governance

A production implementation would need formal policies for:

- retention;
- deletion;
- access logging;
- data minimization;
- encryption;
- secret management;
- customer-data classification;
- incident response.

Simple hashing is not a complete privacy solution when multiple hashed identifiers can still be linked.

---

## 14. Fairness

Shared-device and shared-address features can correlate with legitimate household or socioeconomic patterns.

The project therefore avoids protected attributes and explicitly includes legitimate household scenarios.

Still, graph-based systems can produce indirect proxies. Production use would require monitoring for disparate false-positive behavior and policy review.

---

## 15. Monitoring and drift

The current project does not implement a full production monitoring stack.

A production system should monitor:

- feature drift;
- missingness drift;
- score distribution drift;
- alert-rate drift;
- false-positive rate from analyst outcomes;
- delayed label performance;
- model version changes;
- merchant-policy changes.

An offline benchmark cannot replace continuous post-deployment monitoring.

---

## 16. Evaluation gaps

The current evaluator reports a strong and useful set of classification and exposure metrics, but it does not yet persist:

- PR-AUC;
- precision-recall curves;
- a full top-K pending exposure curve;
- alpha sensitivity;
- formal calibration metrics;
- confidence intervals from repeated independent runs.

These gaps are intentionally disclosed rather than filled with invented values.

---

## 17. What we would build next

The next production-oriented increments would be:

1. **Real labeled merchant data** with rigorous time-based and merchant-disjoint evaluation.
2. **Calibration and operating-point optimization** based on merchant cost tolerance.
3. **Broader graph coverage** with high-degree identifier controls.
4. **Online feature computation and monitoring.**
5. **Analyst feedback loop** for confirmed / dismissed cases.
6. **Formal top-K exposure optimization** under analyst capacity.
7. **Production identity and authorization.**
8. **Durable observability and model governance.**
9. **Adversarial evaluation against adaptive behavior.**
10. **Only then, more complex graph / sequence models if the benchmark shows a material gap.**

---

## Honest final position

Refund Sentinel is best described as:

> **A controlled, auditable proof-of-concept demonstrating that refund-lifecycle and behavioral confirmation can make cross-account refund-abuse detection more useful than either individual-only or structure-only reasoning on a synthetic held-out benchmark.**

That statement is strong without pretending to prove more than the current evidence supports.
